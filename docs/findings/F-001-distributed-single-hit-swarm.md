# F-001 — One client string, many addresses, one hit each: a distributed swarm that looks like nothing at all

*Status: draft, accumulating · Experiment: observational · Seed: 2026.09.0 · Heuristics: v3 at observation, v4 adds the per-session rule · Window: 2026-09-06 01:10 → 2026-09-10 22:40 UTC (five days, two swarms; extend as data arrives) · Report: none yet · Bundle sha256: none yet*

> Draft. The numbers below are one day of real traffic read from an internal-level snapshot by hand. Nothing here is published until a bundle exists and the window is longer than a day. Network prefixes are internal-level data and are deliberately not listed; the counts are.

## Question

Does a crawler that spends its requests one per address, from a large pool of addresses, register in a pipeline whose unit of analysis is the session? Answer so far: not on its own. Every member scored `unknown`, the actor fingerprint split them by prefix, and pairwise clustering had nothing to pair. This note records what the swarm looked like, how it was found, what was added so the console sees the next one, and what to collect before any of this is claimed.

## Setup

Nothing was varied. The mirror ran with seed 2026.09.0 and the seven active experiments; the swarm reached experiment arms like any other visitor. Detection was by hand: a snapshot of the live database was queried for sessions grouped by exact user-agent hash. The by-hand query is in `docs/QUERIES.md` ("Swarm candidates"); the automated version is `src/telemetry/swarm.ts`, which now runs every five minutes.

What counts as a member: `synthetic = 0` and the user-agent string exactly equal to the one below. What counts as the swarm: the set of those sessions in the window.

Per-session scoring under heuristics v3: 93 members `unknown` (the `tiny` rule, by design), 6 `human_browser` (cookie returned, few pages, no machine fetches), 1 `search_bot`. None of the 96 fetched a stylesheet; the six that scored human did so on the strength of a returned cookie and a short page list.

Behavioural clustering (`src/telemetry/cluster.ts`, 24 h window, pairwise similarity ≥ 0.62) produced no group containing more than a handful of members: two one-request sessions share a user-agent and nothing else.

The pattern was still present at the end of the window and had not changed shape.

Three details read from the same snapshot after the first draft:

- **The frontier saturated within the day.** Per hour, the share of swarm page requests that hit a page the pool had never fetched fell from about half in the first hours to zero from 18:00 UTC on; the last four active hours were all re-fetches of pages already seen (Main_Page, Special:UserLogin, Legacy_Toolchain lead). 33 distinct pages after 129 requests, on a mirror with about 60 articles plus specials.
- **Every internal Referer pointed at a hub, not a page.** The 27 referers were the index stub, the archive stub, the root, and Special:Random. No request carried a page-to-page referer, so the pool's URLs appear to be handed out from hub fetches rather than discovered one page from the last. A page-to-page frontier lag cannot be measured yet.
- **No conditional requests.** The mirror did not emit `ETag` or `Last-Modified` until 2026-09-07, and no swarm request carried `If-None-Match` or `If-Modified-Since` (one such request exists in the whole database, from an unrelated session). The mirror now tags stable documents and answers 304; the swarm page tracks the share of members that revalidate.
- **Frontier lag through the link graph, first reading.** Taking the seed's visible links as parent → child edges (the pool sends no page-to-page referers), 15 of the 30 pages had a measurable lag between the pool's last fetch of a linking page and its first fetch of the linked one: median 29 minutes, quartiles 13 and 52 minutes, the shortest 3 minutes (two children of `Main_Page`). Slower and wider than the 10-minute rhythm of new addresses, which says the scheduler's queue is not "fetch what the last page linked to, next tick". The swarm page in the console now computes this continuously.
- **Second day, read live at 2026-09-07 05:00 UTC.** 121 sessions, 101 prefixes, 146 requests, 38 pages. The pool has reached the JSON-LD-only page (4 members) as well as the two `og_only` pages (2 members), and no other client family has reached either: the three declared crawlers on the mirror found their hidden pages through the sitemap, the stale index, the feed and an HTML comment, never through head metadata. With its frontier exhausted the pool has started fetching `?action=history` and `?action=edit` views of pages it already holds. The first SGX-011 v2 carriers went out during the night: two members were shown the `og:image` and JSON-LD carriers, one declared crawler the bare head text, one browser the HTML comment; nothing has fetched the target yet. The pool's last hit came at 05:15 UTC after 130 sessions and 108 prefixes; by 08:50 UTC it had been silent for three and a half hours, past the previous day's longest lull, with the edge verified up and the rest of the site's traffic at its lowest. It resumed at 09:08 UTC after three hours and fifty minutes, two members fetching `Main_Page` from two new prefixes, so the pool pauses rather than stops. Laid end to end, the gaps between member arrivals show the schedule: five pauses in the first 44 hours (88, 193, 73, 233 and 246 minutes) and, between them, four working runs of six hours each to within two minutes (06:39 to 12:41, 15:54 to 21:58, 23:11 to 05:15 and 09:08 to 15:12 UTC). Inside a run arrivals come every 9.9 minutes at the median, 7.3 to 12.0 between the 10th and 90th percentile, about 36 requests per run. A fixed six-hour work window with a variable rest between windows is a scheduler's shape, not a queue draining; if it holds, the run that began at 19:18 on 2026-09-07 ends near 01:20 on the 8th. The mix shifted with saturation: a third of first-day requests carried a query string, half of second-day requests did, and the pool fetched five `Special:Export` documents that robots.txt disallows, consistent with never having read it. The site's edge was verified reachable from outside during the third pause. A single pass followed by lasting silence would be the snapshot-corpus shape listed under "what to collect next"; this was not that. The first member back was shown the real `og:see_also` carrier, the second `og:image`.
- **First tagged fetch, 2026-09-07 09:42 UTC.** The `og:image` carrier's URL, shown exactly once, at 09:08:50 to a member on one prefix, was fetched 33 minutes later by a member on a different prefix that had never fetched `Main_Page`. One request, no Referer, no cookie, the pool's usual document `Accept` header, the same `zh-CN` language and header order as every other member. Three things follow. The handoff of URLs between addresses is now observed directly rather than inferred, and its lag matched the link-graph estimate. The pool takes URL-shaped content out of `og:` properties without regard to what the property means: an `og:image` that points at an HTML page was fetched as a page, which a client that understood Open Graph would not do. And the fetching session's own arm (G, canonical) says nothing about how it got there, which is the whole reason the arm rides in the URL; the event's `discover_class` was stamped from the fetcher's arm and reads `link_only` where it should read `og_only`, a leak fixed the same morning.
- **Second and third tagged fetches, 10:41 and 10:52 UTC.** The same `og:image` URL was fetched again from a third address, 93 minutes after its single exposure and 59 minutes after its first fetch, with no conditional headers although the first fetch had been given an `ETag`: the pool re-fetches URLs it already holds, and re-fetches them blind. Then the bare-text carrier's URL, a string sitting in `<head>` inside no element at all, shown once to a member at 09:51, was fetched at 10:52 from another address that had never seen the host page. A correction made the same afternoon: text inside `<head>` is not head content to an HTML5 parser, which closes the head at the first non-whitespace character and hands the string to the body, so what this fetch shows is that the reader linkifies bare URLs in text, not that it scans raw source. Which carriers it does not take is the sharper question. By 13:45 UTC the pool had been shown, and had not followed, the real `og:see_also` (shown 09:12), the fake-namespace `see_also` (04:32 and 09:22), a second `og:url` (04:25), the JSON-LD `relatedLink` (05:03) and the HTML comment (04:04), against `og:image` followed in 33 minutes and the bare text in 61. Four of those exposures fell in the hour before the pool's four-hour pause and may have been lost rather than declined; the `og:see_also` and fake `see_also` shown after it resumed are the clean cases, and one exposure each is not a result. The asymmetry that looked most telling, `og:image` taken and `og:see_also` left, did not hold up: at 19:33 UTC, fifteen minutes after the pool came back from a four-hour pause and a member was shown it, the arbitrary `<meta name="antfarm-related" content="…">` carrier was fetched from another address. A made-up meta name with a URL in it is what a generic attribute miner takes and what nothing else does, so the reader mines URL-shaped attributes whatever they are called. The untaken `og:see_also` and fake `see_also` remain single exposures, and the pool visibly samples rather than exhausts what it sees (a quarter of its URLs get the hourly echo, the rest none), so one exposure not followed is weak evidence while one followed is strong. By 21:00 UTC the pool had been shown eight of the eleven carriers (never the canonical link, and the alternate link only by a scanner) and had followed three, `og:image`, the bare text and the arbitrary meta name, in 33, 61 and 15 minutes. The five it had not followed were each shown once, and two of those exposures, `og:see_also` at 09:12 and the fake `see_also` at 09:22, fell in the same run and minutes apart from two it did follow, so run boundaries do not explain the difference. A pool that takes a random quarter to a third of the links on each page it fetches would produce exactly this table, three of eight, and an hourly echo on a quarter of its URLs. Until each arm has several exposures the result is "it mines URL-shaped attributes and text" and nothing about what it declines. The exposure rate is the bottleneck: a member renders the host page about once an hour, so carrying the carrier on every article the pool fetches would multiply exposures by five. Done at 21:14 UTC on the 7th: the carrier now rides on every article except the experiment targets, the earlier host stays on record for the events before the switch, and the version and the revision ids are unchanged so the URLs already in the pool's queue keep their meaning. The first result under the widened host arrived within two hours: a member shown the second `og:url` carrier on a user page at 23:12 UTC, and at 00:13 the target was fetched from another address carrying that arm's id. Four carriers followed now, all four of them URL-shaped attribute values (`og:image`, `og:url`, an invented `meta name`, and a bare URL in text), against nothing yet from the two `see_also` properties, the JSON-LD block, the two `link` elements or the HTML comment. The exposure rate did not multiply as expected, because the pool spends most of a run on furniture rather than article renders: three page renders in the first five hours after the switch. Ordinary browser and crawler traffic supplies the rest, so arms fill unevenly.

Then at 02:05 UTC on the 8th the second `<link rel="canonical">` was followed, three minutes after a member was shown it, the fastest handoff recorded. That breaks the attributes-only reading: the pool takes an `href` off a `link` element too. Five carriers followed, and the pattern that survives is a client that resolves URLs from head metadata of several kinds while leaving alone, so far, the two `see_also` properties, the JSON-LD block, the `link rel="alternate"` and the HTML comment. Given the sampling, "so far" is doing real work in that sentence: `og:see_also` has had two exposures and the comment two, against one or two for most of the carriers that were taken.

The canonical URL then behaved unlike any other. From one exposure at 02:02 it was fetched four times in 63 minutes, at 02:05, 02:35, 02:55 and 03:08, each from a different address, none carrying a conditional header. Every other tagged URL has been fetched once or twice on the hourly echo. The difference plausibly lies in what a canonical link means: a crawler that treats it as the page's preferred address may re-resolve it whenever the host page comes up in its queue, so the same URL is re-enqueued rather than deduplicated. If so, `rel="canonical"` is a way to make this pool spend requests, and the mirror's own canonical tags are worth checking for a loop of the same kind.

That reading did not survive. The target's own first revision, an ordinary permalink the pool took from the page's history view, then repeated the pattern five times in an hour, and after an eight-and-a-half-hour silence the pool returned at 15:46 UTC on the 8th, fetched `Main_Page` once, and spent its next four requests on the same carrier URL at ten-minute intervals. What the three bursts share is not the carrier's kind but the shape: a URL is resolved, then re-fetched several times at the pool's own arrival cadence, each time from a different address, never conditionally, then dropped. It looks less like a refresh schedule than like several members being handed the same queue entry. A wiki, which gives every page many addresses, feeds that behaviour indefinitely.

The sixth carrier followed is the fake `antfarm:see_also` property, taken in those four fetches. Two `see_also` properties were the last untaken metadata carriers of their kind, and the invented one has now gone; only the real `og:see_also`, the JSON-LD block, the `link rel="alternate"` and the HTML comment remain unfetched, each on one or two exposures.
- **Fourth and fifth fetches, 11:41 and 11:51 UTC, and the hourly echo.** The head-text URL was fetched a second time 59 minutes after the first, from yet another address, exactly as the `og:image` URL had been. In between, an address fetched the target's own permalink (`?oldid=9`, the mirror's route number), a link that exists only in the target page's HTML: the target is inside the pool's frontier now, and that fetch carries no carrier id by design, so the comparison reports it as unattributed. The 59-minute spacing is not the target's alone. Of the 99 distinct URLs the pool had fetched by noon, 24 were fetched again, and 19 of those second fetches came 57 to 63 minutes after the first; three URLs got a third fetch another hour later; the same pairs are there on the first day. So roughly a quarter of what the pool fetches is fetched once more, from a different address, one hour on, with no conditional headers. A second fetch from a second address an hour later is how a crawler checks whether a page is stable, cloaked or personalised. If that is what this is, per-actor experiment arms make the mirror look unstable to it: the two addresses draw different arms and see different head markup on the host page. Whether the re-fetched quarter is chosen or sampled is open.

**Fifth day, read live at 2026-09-10 23:00 UTC. Every carrier has been taken, and the control has never been touched.** The two that held out longest went on the 10th: the real `og:see_also` at 02:45 and the `link rel="alternate"` at 15:36. That completes the table.

The control is the line that matters most. Across five days and 87 sessions shown a stimulus, nothing has ever fetched the target without a carrier id in the url, so the target is reachable only through something that names it and the other ten rows are not measuring background curiosity. What the ten rows say together is that the reader takes URL-shaped strings out of head metadata without regard to the format carrying them: a real Open Graph relationship property, an invented namespace, an arbitrary meta name, two `link` elements, a JSON-LD block, bare text and an html comment all worked. The comment is the sharpest of them, because comment content never enters the DOM as text or as a link, so nothing walking a parsed document can see it; the earlier bare-text result did not prove raw-source reading, since an HTML5 parser relocates stray head text into the body, but a comment has no such escape hatch. The counts are exposure-limited and are not a preference ordering: C and D sit at one because they have had one or two exposures, not because they are less attractive.

**The re-fetch bursts stop at six, per fleet.** Every burst SW-E6F2B0 has finished came to exactly six fetches, on six separate carriers, each fetch from a different address at the pool's own nine-to-ten-minute arrival cadence, none of them conditional. The two rows above six are not counter-examples: G's seventh fetch came from a session in no cluster and H's from the other swarm, so within one fleet the ceiling has held at six every time it has been reached. Arms C, D, E and I are incomplete bursts rather than short ones, and bursts survive pauses, so the prediction is that they reach six and stop. On 2026-09-09 the H burst also produced the first fetch by an address that had itself been shown the carrier, and it arrived fourth of six, thirty-two minutes after that address rendered the host page and after three other addresses had already fetched it. The nineteen fetches before it all came from addresses that never saw the stimulus. Taken together that is a queue and not a crawler following its own links: the address that discovers a url has no special claim on it and turns up later as an ordinary worker.

**A second swarm, and this one is a declared crawler that checks out.** SW-5DA754 appeared at 2026-09-09 22:20: 68 sessions, 68 requests, 62 network prefixes across 54 wider blocks, one or two requests per session, one asset fetch in the whole run, no conditional requests, 35 pages. It declares Amazonbot. Checked by hand on 2026-09-10 against Amazon's published crawler address list, all 62 of its prefixes contain a published Amazonbot address; measured against the size of that provider's address space in the region, 62 unrelated prefixes landing that way is not a coincidence worth entertaining. This is Amazon's crawler. The project stores neither the provider nor the addresses and this note lists neither; the check is a researcher's, repeatable from the same public list.

The two swarms trip the same detector on very different shapes. SW-E6F2B0 spreads 170 prefixes over 31 wider blocks and arrives on a ten-minute metronome; SW-5DA754 spreads 62 prefixes over 54 blocks and moves in bursts of three or four requests seconds apart with forty-minute pauses between them, median gap 4.1 seconds. What they share is the crawl vocabulary: both walk `action=edit&section=N`, `action=info`, `action=history`, `Special:UserLogin`, `Special:AllPages`, `Special:WhatLinksHere`, `Special:RecentChangesLinked`, `Special:Random`, the feed, and the archive and index stubs, and both 404 on `Talk:` pages that do not exist. That is either what generic wiki crawlers do or a shared lineage, and this dataset cannot tell which.

**The declared crawler never asked for this host's robots.txt, and behaved as though it had read it.** In 68 sessions over 24 hours SW-5DA754 made no request for `/robots.txt` or `/sitemap.xml`. The app records those fetches from every other client family, so the absence is real and not a logging gap. It also never touched a disallowed path, where SW-E6F2B0 has hit them eleven times, mostly `Special:Export`; and its pacing sits above the `Crawl-delay: 2` the file declares, with a minimum gap between distinct urls of 2.29 seconds and a tenth percentile of 2.59, against 0.51 seconds for the pool that does ignore the file. Its four sub-second gaps are all redirect pairs, one logical fetch each. Three observations pointing the same way: it is working from this host's rules without having asked us for them. Amazon's own documentation leaves that open, saying its crawlers fetch host-level robots.txt or use a cached copy from the last thirty days, and the hostname resolved before the mirror went live, so a probe predating our logging would still be inside that window. What cannot be said from this data is that Amazonbot ignores robots.txt: it never read the file here and it never broke it, and those are different claims. SGX-012, below, is the test that separates them.

**The arm token identifies the arm and not the exposure, and the second swarm is what exposed that.** Two of SW-5DA754's fetches carried SGX-011 ids, which looked at first like one fleet inheriting the other's queue. It is not: members of SW-5DA754 had been assigned those arms and had rendered host pages carrying them before each fetch, at 23:23 and 03:35 for the JSON-LD id fetched at 10:03, and at 04:23 for the `og:image` id fetched at 12:10. The innocent reading holds. The point is that no reading could have been ruled out, because the id is a per-arm constant: two fleets drawing the same arm are served byte-identical urls, so the schema cannot separate a url inherited from another fleet from one found independently. Catching a genuine cross-fleet handoff needs a nonce minted per exposure and recorded against the session that was shown it. That is a limit of the v2 design rather than of the data.

## What this supports

- A client pool can spend one request per address, from many addresses, at a rate that never trips a per-address limit, and be invisible to session-level scoring. The session is the wrong unit for this behaviour; the client signature across sessions is the right one.
- The pool follows internal links (Referer values point at the mirror's own index and archive stubs and at Special:Random), reads OpenGraph metadata (two `og_only` pages were reached, which are listed nowhere else), and occasionally takes feeds and text alternates. It does not read robots.txt.
- The pool does not render: zero asset fetches across 119 requests from a client that claims to be Mobile Safari.
- `Pragma` on every request separates this client from real browsers in this dataset: 89 of the 93 swarm sessions versus 1 of 16 real Chrome sessions (a hard-reload visit).
- Cookies are honoured per address (23 of 23 valid on return) but no cookie ever moved between prefixes: every multi-request session had one actor fingerprint.
- A carrier experiment needs its empty arm. Ten carriers were followed and the no-carrier control was fetched zero times in five days, which is the only reason the ten can be read as reach rather than as noise.
- The reader takes URL-shaped strings out of head metadata regardless of the format around them, including an html comment, whose content no parsed-document walker can see. Discovery reads the bytes.
- Discovery and fetching are separate stages sharing a queue. Twenty of twenty-one tagged fetches came from addresses that had never been shown the stimulus, and the one exception was the discovering address arriving fourth in its own burst.
- The detector's signals — one client signature over many prefixes, one hit per address, nothing rendered, a sustained trickle — do not distinguish a covert pool from a legitimate large-scale crawler. SW-5DA754 scores as high as SW-E6F2B0 on all four and is verifiably Amazon's declared crawler. The signals say "distributed fleet", never "hostile".

## Speculation

Not phones. Ninety-six devices on a six-year-old iOS build, all requesting from cloud address ranges, all Chinese-language, none rendering, is one program with a pool of egress addresses. The one-hit-per-address pattern reads as a design goal rather than an accident: it defeats per-address rate limits and, as it happens, session-based analysis.

The address ranges are, by hand against public registry data, all allocated to a single cloud provider (Tencent Cloud's international ranges). The project does not store ASN and this note does not list prefixes; the attribution is a researcher's observation, not a stored field, and should be re-checked on each extension of the window. The same client string on the same provider's ranges, one hit per address, has been described publicly since 2024 by site operators and in at least one TLS-fingerprinting write-up. Who runs it has not been publicly established. The plausible reading is corpus collection for search or model training without disclosure; the language header is the loudest hint about where the operator sits, and hints are all this is.

The `og_only` reaches are the most interesting behavioural detail: the client parses `<meta property="og:see_also">`, which ordinary link extractors ignore. That is a deliberate feature, not a generic crawler default. Whether it understands Open Graph or simply harvests any URL-shaped `content=` attribute is an open question with an experiment attached (below). On the second day the pool also reached the JSON-LD-only page, which rules out "Open Graph reader and nothing else": it takes URLs out of at least two kinds of head metadata that no declared crawler on the mirror has touched. The header-only and link-only pages remain unreached, so the width of the net is still open. SGX-011's first result points the same way as the JSON-LD reach: the URL inside an `og:image` property was fetched as a document, so the reader is a URL harvester over metadata rather than a consumer of what the metadata says. The bare-text carrier, followed an hour after it was shown, adds that it linkifies URLs it finds in text. The arbitrary-meta carrier, taken fifteen minutes after it was shown, settles that it mines URL-shaped attributes whatever their name. What it declines, if anything, needs more than one exposure per arm to say, because the pool samples rather than exhausts what it sees.

The user-agent string is not a chosen disguise. It was the Chromium DevTools device-emulation preset for iPhone models, and the descriptor that Puppeteer-style tooling ships under names like "iPhone X", until a DevTools commit on 2023-09-12 ("Update device emulation user agent strings") moved the presets to iOS 16.6. Someone copied an emulation preset years ago and never updated it. The client behind the string is not Chromium, though: every Chromium since 2019 sends `Sec-Fetch-*` headers on navigations and these requests carry none, and the header order (`host, user-agent, accept, accept-encoding, accept-language, cache-control, pragma, upgrade-insecure-requests`) is not Chromium's. The `Cache-Control: no-cache` plus `Pragma: no-cache` pair is what Chrome sends with caching disabled, which fits the same story: an HTTP library carrying a copied browser fingerprint, headers included.

External corroboration, checked on 2026-09-07: BotConduct's April 2026 report (part 2, actor profiles) describes the same string from Tencent Cloud ASN 132203, 194 addresses, 362 requests over six days, 1.8 requests per address, never reading robots.txt, and declines to attribute the operator beyond "any Tencent Cloud customer". Same client, same pool, five months earlier, on someone else's site.

The second swarm changes what the first one means. Before SW-5DA754 there was one distributed fleet on this mirror and every coordination signal it tripped could be read as evidence of something covert. Now there are two, they trip the same four signals, and one of them is Amazon's, publicly documented, verifiable from a published address list, and pacing itself to a crawl-delay it never asked us for. Distributed single-hit crawling is not the tell it looked like on day one; it is how large fleets crawl. What still separates SW-E6F2B0 is everything around the shape rather than the shape itself: a user-agent copied from a browser emulation preset, no declared identity, no published ranges, no robots.txt fetch and eleven disallowed-path hits, a language header pointing somewhere specific, and a six-hour shift pattern with variable rests. Those are the grounds for treating it as undeclared, not the prefix count.

## Caveats specific to this finding

- Two swarms is not a sample. Everything above comparing SW-E6F2B0 and SW-5DA754 is a comparison of two things, and the thresholds in `src/telemetry/swarm.ts` are now tuned against two examples instead of one. Whether they would catch a slower or smaller third fleet is still untested; both known swarms clear them comfortably.
- The SGX-011 arm id is a per-arm constant, so two fleets assigned the same arm receive identical urls and the schema cannot tell an inherited url from an independently discovered one. Every cross-fleet claim in this note rests on exposure timestamps, checked case by case, not on the id.
- Six-per-fleet rests on six completed bursts from one fleet. Arms C, D, E and I are still in flight and would falsify it by stopping somewhere else.
- The Amazonbot verification is a /24-level match: the project stores truncated prefixes, so what was checked is that each prefix contains a published address, not that the requesting address was itself on the list. At 62 of 62 the conclusion is safe, but a single prefix would prove nothing.
- Amazonbot's apparent robots compliance is inference from three indirect signals, not an observation of it reading the file. It may equally have no rules for this host and simply not have reached a disallowed path in 68 requests. SGX-012 exists because this note cannot settle that.
- The per-day tables in `What we observed` are each a single day read by hand, and the first-day rates in particular were written when one day was all there was. Read them as dated entries, not as properties of the pool.
- Six members scored `human_browser` under v3. Heuristics v4 (`pragma_no_render`) removes that; the table above is the v3 record and is kept as such.
- Sessions are keyed by cookie, then by prefix plus user-agent. A pool that shared a cookie jar across addresses would collapse into fewer, longer sessions and look entirely different. This one did not; a later one might.
- The swarm detector's thresholds (12 sessions, 8 prefixes, 60% one-hit, under 5% assets) were chosen against this single example and the first day's real browser traffic. A second swarm arrived on 2026-09-09 and clears all four comfortably on a completely different rhythm, which tests the thresholds' floor not at all: what is still unknown is what they miss.
- Canary exposure is the lever here, and it is the one measurement that has grown teeth. By 2026-09-10 the mirror had issued 8,474 canary ids in html comments to 1,001 sessions, 5,640 each in headers, meta tags and visible text to 803, and 5,403 in JSON-LD to 791, of which 180 were shown only to members of SW-E6F2B0. Not one has been sighted: no cross-session reappearance, no external sighting, nothing. If any of them turns up in a search index, a model's answer or a third-party fetch, that sighting attributes the pool's purpose; until then the purpose is speculation, and five days of silence is a stronger negative than one day of it but still only a negative.

- Robots-compliance experiment (SGX-012, built 2026-09-11). Two fresh twin pages, `Rebuild_Queue_Notes_2016` and `Rebuild_Queue_Drafts_2016`, linked side by side on every article, with robots.txt forbidding exactly one of them and the arms swapping which, so a twin left alone can be blamed on the rule rather than on its title or url. Taking both twins means the client is working without rules for this host, which a robots.txt cached from before the twins existed produces exactly as well as ignoring the file does; the experiment cannot separate those two and says so. Taking the allowed twin and leaving the forbidden one is the result worth having, because for a client that never requests robots.txt from us it can only mean current rules arrived by a channel we do not serve. Taking neither says nothing, which is what the allowed twin is for.

- Metadata-carrier experiment (SGX-011, activated 2026-09-07 on `Main_Page`, restarted as v2 the same night): one page exposing an otherwise unlinked target through exactly one carrier per actor: a real `og:see_also`, a fake `antfarm:see_also` property, an arbitrary `<meta content="https://…">`, `og:url`, `og:image`, `<link rel="alternate">`, `<link rel="canonical">`, JSON-LD, bare text in `<head>`, an HTML comment, and a no-carrier control. v1 named the target with the same URL in every arm, and its first six assignments showed the flaw: three were pool members, and by this note's own frontier-lag reading the address that sees a carrier is not the one that fetches what it names, so the fetching session's arm would have said nothing about the carrier. v2 names the target as `?oldid=<six digits>`, a per-arm revision id the mirror answers like any permalink. A fetch is credited to the id it carries whichever address makes it; `cross_actor` counts fetches by actors that never fetched the host page, which is the handoff observed directly; and the time from the arm's latest exposure to the tagged fetch is the handoff lag. Fetches carrying no id (history links, guessed titles, stripped query strings) are reported as unattributed rather than dropped. Which carriers get fetched separates a semantic Open Graph reader from a generic attribute miner from a structured-document ingester.
